<p class="Tuaduoi">Tìm hiểu luật Mỹ:</p>



  

<p><span class="First">T</span>heo GS Luật <b><i>Chris Edelson </i></b>của American University:  </p>
<p>Hiến pháp Mỹ <i>không nói rõ </i> về việc giao cho Tổng Thống Mỹ quyền ban hành tình trạng quốc gia nguy cấp,  mà nói rõ về việc <i>giao quyền đó cho Quốc  Hội.</i>  Nhưng Quốc Hội đã từng ủy quyền cho TT làm chuyện đó,   trong khi vẫn giữ <b><i>quyền  bác bỏ</i></b> tuyên bố "<i>quốc gia nguy cấp</i>" của TT. </p>
<p><b>Khi Nội Chiến Mỹ khởi đầu</b>, TT  Lincoln lợi dụng lúc QH không nhóm họp    để ban hành <b><i>quyền bắt giữ kẻ gian phản quốc</i></b> mà<i> không qua xét xử, </i>    và khi QH nhóm họp thì ông xin, và  được QH cho phép dùng quyền ấy. </p>
<p><b>Năm 1952,</b> công nhân các hãng sản  xuất thép đình công,    TT Truman, <b><i>viện cớ</i></b> chiến tranh Triều Tiên,    ban hành tình trạng quốc gia nguy cấp    để dành quyền kiểm soát các hãng thép    - trong khi <b><i>Quốc Hội lờ đi,</i></b> không can dự.      Các ông chủ hãng thép kiện lên Tòa Tối Cao,    tòa này xử <b><i>TT Mỹ sai,</i></b> với số phiếu 6-3,    vì lí do TT đã<i> không dựa theo khuôn khổ  luật pháp liên hệ </i>   - mà nếu TT tự ý làm vậy thì quyền hành tập  trung quá lớn trong tay TT,    điều mà các lãnh đạo sáng lập Hiến pháp Mỹ không muốn . </p>
<p><b>Năm 1976,</b> Quốc Hội Mỹ ban hành Đạo  Luật Quốc Gia Nguy Cấp,    tuy với chủ ý là <b><i>giới hạn quyền hành TT </i></b>trong các  trường hợp đó,    nhưng vì lí do tranh đấu chính trị trong  QH,    đã<i> thả lỏng cho TT hành xử đơn phương -  không có giám sát</i>.    Như vậy, <i>QH đã không làm tròn nhiệm vụ bảo vệ luật pháp .</i>  </p>
<p><b>Đạo luật năm 1976,</b> The National  Emergency Act of 1976, <b><i>cho phép TT ban hành</i></b> tình trạng quốc gia nguy cấp    - mà <i>không cần sự phê duyệt  đồng  thuận</i> của QH,    và đó là luật và quyền hành do QH tự tạo ra, <i>không  có trong Hiến pháp !</i>   (người Mỹ gọi đó là "<i>statute</i>", thời VNCH có từ "<i>đạo dụ</i>").      Theo luật <b><i>ngoài hiến pháp</i></b> này,    các TT Mỹ đã ban hành <b><i>31 luật</i></b> <i>viện lí do nguy cấp quốc gia </i>   như cấm vận kinh tế các phần tử khủng bố     hay kiểm soát các tàu bè ngoại bang  trong lãnh hải Mỹ. </p>
<p>Đối với những đạo luật <i>đặc biệt ngoài hiến pháp</i> như thế,    QH có quyền bỏ phiếu phủ quyết, hay <b>duyệt xét  định kỳ</b> mỗi 6 tháng, <i>*** NHƯNG  NÓ KHÔNG BAO GIỜ LÀM VẬY !</i> *** </p>

<p>Đặc biệt quan trọng cho Donald  Trump,    Đạo Luật Quốc Gia Nguy Cấp Năm 1976    <i>không hề đặt ra tiêu chuẩn </i>để  định đoạt <b><i>thế nào là "</i></b><i>quốc gia. nguy cấp" !</i>      Nếu Trump ban hành tình trạng quốc gia nguy cấp,    thì các tòa án <i>không có tiêu chuẩn rõ rệt  nào </i>để phủ bác.      Ngược lại, chỉ có QH mới phủ quyết được tình trạng này,    nhưng Trump sẽ dùng  <b><i>quyền phủ quyết</i></b> (veto) của  TT,    sau đó QH phải hội đủ 2/3 số phiếu để "dập tắt" phủ quyết của  TT    - và đó sẽ là chuyện <i>không hề dễ dàng, </i>    <i>trong  tình trạng chính trị nhiễu loạn </i>hiện nay ! </p>
<p><b><i>Nếu QH bất lực </i></b>thì TT sẽ <i>lạm  quyền, </i>   mà không có cách nào khác để thách thức quyền lực ấy.      Trump sẽ "thừa thắng xông lên", được đà xông tới :   ban hành<b><i> tình trạng quốc gia nguy cấp </i></b>   <i>không chỉ </i>để "cướp  tiền" xây tường <i>phung phí vô hiệu quả,</i>    mà còn<b><i> có thể </i></b><i>giới hạn quyền tự do chống đối </i>    - như <i>đóng cửa nhà báo và nhốt tù bịt miệng</i> những  ai chỉ trích y ! </p>

<p>Tường rào <b><i>đã  có sẵn</i></b> nhưng Trump tự tạo "tình trạng nguy cấp"    để <i>bắt nhà nước làm con tin, bắt chẹt, tống  tiền, chơi trò quyền lực !</i></p>
<p align="center"><img src="https://pub-756b239a5ae84d4c8c8ff709f375d430.r2.dev/THOISU_CT/Hinh/HOAKY/TRUMPIST/BorderWall_Tijuana_San_Diego.jpg" width="350" height="200" alt=""></p>

<p>If President Donald Trump <a href="https://www.marketwatch.com/story/trumps-national-emergency-threat-shows-hes-up-against-the-wall-in-border-fight-2019-01-11?mod=mw_share_twitter" target="_blank">declares a national  emergency</a> to fund <i>some portion of a wall</i> along  the U.S.-Mexico border <i>without  congressional authorization,</i> what would happen next?   <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2019/01/08/how-supreme-court-might-rule-if-trump-declares-an-emergency-his-wall/?utm_term=.d675d462039a" target="_blank">Would the courts</a> step  in? What is Congress’ role?   As I explain in my book “<a href="https://uwpress.wisc.edu/books/5067.htm" target="_blank">Emergency Presidential Power</a>,”  presidents generally claim emergency power two ways: through inherent or implied  authority under the U.S. Constitution or <i>under statutory authority</i> (<i>not based on The Constitution,</i> but)  granted by Congress.   Relying on the Constitution as a  basis for emergency power is controversial, and less likely to stand up to  meaningful congressional or judicial review. <i>The U.S. Constitution saysnothing specific  about presidential emergency power: </i>Presidents can only claim  such authority is implied or inherent.   <i>The emergency  powers the Constitution does describe are actually assigned to Congress</i>.  Congress has <b><i>delegated</i></b> some emergency powers to the  president through statutes, including the National Emergencies Act. <i>But Congress retains the power to reject a  president’s declaration of a national emergency.</i>   If President Trump does declare an  emergency, the question is: <i>Will Congress use the power available to  it, or will it play the role of passive spectator</i>?   <b>Gaining  congressional approval</b>   Since presidents lack any specific  constitutional emergency power, they often find it necessary to gain congressional  authorization. For instance, <i>at the  start of the Civil War, with Congress out of session, President Abraham Lincoln  suspended<b>habeas corpus</b> and took other unilateral actions. He later  sought and gained retroactive <a href="https://newrepublic.com/article/62296/lincoln-v-lincoln" target="_blank">approval from Congress</a> for  these actions.</i>   This precedent of gaining  congressional approval was put to the test nearly 100 years later.<i> In 1952, <a href="https://www.american.edu/spa/news/presidential-power-since-sept11-09082016.cfm" target="_blank">President Harry  Truman</a>claimed emergency power to take control of steel factories  during the Korean War in response to a labor strike. <a href="https://muse.jhu.edu/book/27307" target="_blank">He invoked</a> a “very great inherent power to  meet great national emergencies.” Congress took no specific action to approve  or disapprove, </i>though a pre-existing statute on the books  weighed against Truman. </p>
<p><i>When  factory owners <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/history/2019/01/08/truman-declared-an-emergency-when-he-felt-thwarted-trump-should-know-it-didnt-end-well/?utm_term=.8a977e780d83" target="_blank">sued the  administration</a>, the Supreme Court, by a 6-3 vote, ruled against  Truman in the famous <a href="https://muse.jhu.edu/book/27307" target="_blank">Youngstown Sheet  decision</a>.</i></p>
<p>Justice  Robert H. Jackson’s concurring opinion in that case has been <a href="https://www.roberthjackson.org/opinion/concurring-opinion-youngstown-v-sawyer-343-u-s-579-june-2-1952/" target="_blank">especially  influential</a> and is often cited by legal scholars and judges. He  outlined a three-part test to be used as a starting point in determining when  presidential action is constitutionally permissible.   Under  Jackson’s test, presidents are on the strongest possible footing when acting  with congressional approval. In this case, Jackson said, <i>Truman’s position was weak  since he was taking action that did not comply with the relevant legislative  framework. In Jackson’s view, Truman’s reliance on inherent emergency power  under the Constitution <b>would dangerously concentrate power in the  president’s hands, </b>something the framers would not have wanted.</i>    <b>Congress’s role</b>   Jackson’s  opinion in Youngstown suggested that <b><i>emergency power could be  defined by Congress in statutes.</i></b>   Congress  took up that suggestion with the <a href="https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2019/01/presidential-emergency-powers/576418/" target="_blank">National Emergencies  Act of 1976</a>. Though the act was designed to set limits on presidential  power to declare national emergencies of indefinite length, it has ended  up <i>providing  a largely unregulated way</i> for presidents to take<i> unilateral </i>action. <b><i>Congress  has failed to fulfill its responsibilities under the law</i></b>.   The  National Emergencies Act permits the president to declare a national emergency <i>without congressional  approval, </i>triggering  specific <b><i>statutory powers</i></b> that the president can use.  For instance, presidents have used this law to <a href="https://www.lawfareblog.com/emergencies-without-end-primer-federal-states-emergency" target="_blank">impose economic  sanctions</a> against terrorists after 9/11 or regulate foreign  ships in U.S. waters. Thirty-one <a href="https://pub-756b239a5ae84d4c8c8ff709f375d430.r2.devhttps://www.brennancenter.org/sites/default/files/analysis/NEA%20Declarations.pdf" target="_blank">emergency  declarations</a> are currently in effect under the statute.    Congress  can vote at any time to terminate a state of emergency, and is required by the  statute to meet every six months while an emergency is in effect to consider  whether it should continue. However, <b><i>it has never voted on an  emergency declared by a president</i></b> <b><i>or held meetings as  required </i></b>by the statute.    Perhaps  most importantly for Trump, the National Emergencies Act provides <i>no criteria for deciding  whether a national emergency exists</i>. We know from history that presidents can  contrive emergencies as a pretext for action.   For  example, in 1846 President James Polk <a href="https://books.google.com/books?id=4vPPAQAAQBAJ&amp;pg=PR11&amp;lpg=PR11&amp;dq=james+polk+mexico+chris+edelson&amp;source=bl&amp;ots=Y6ZMQxgeXn&amp;sig=BJ4G6XmRoIhg2usQeBnXJM7osRo&amp;hl=en&amp;sa=X&amp;ved=2ahUKEwjX1JrZsebfAhUQjlkKHVosCCYQ6AEwAnoECAAQAQ#v=onepage&amp;q=james%20polk%20mexico%20chris%20edelson&amp;f=false" target="_blank">falsely claimed that  Mexico</a> had spilled American blood on U.S. soil as a pretext for  gaining a declaration of war from Congress.   In  1942, President Franklin D. Roosevelt justified the decision to intern 110,000  Japanese-Americans without trial <a href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/injustice-japanese-americans-internment-camps-resonates-strongly-180961422/" target="_blank">based on false claims</a> that  time was of the essence, and at least some Japanese-Americans were known to be  disloyal.   Although  both of these examples pre-date the 1976 Act, they serve as cautionary tales  about the wisdom of accepting at face value a president’s claim that an  emergency exists. However, because the law now in effect provides no specific  standards to define the existence of an emergency, courts might be inclined to  defer to presidential discretion. If President Trump declares a national  emergency at the border, it is <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2019/01/08/how-supreme-court-might-rule-if-trump-declares-an-emergency-his-wall/?utm_term=.ed598aa772fe" target="_blank">far from clear that  courts would strike it down</a>. </p>
<p>By  contrast, <i>it  would be straightforward for Congress to reverse a declaration of national  emergency. The National Emergencies Act gives legislators authority to reject a  presidential declaration of national emergency through <a href="https://www.vox.com/policy-and-politics/2019/1/11/18178438/national-emergency-marco-rubio-precedent-democrats-climate" target="_blank">simple legislation</a> that  would require majorities in the House and Senate. President Trump would  presumably veto such action. Legislators would have the opportunity to override  a presidential veto with a two-thirds majority vote. That of course would be no  easy task in the current Congress.</i>    Because  of the way the National Emergencies Act was drafted, Congress is better  positioned to take action than the courts – assuming enough members are moved  to act.<i> If Congress does nothing, then  the law could become a vehicle for <b>presidential abuse,</b> especially  because the act’s language seems to grant the president broad discretion that  could <b>insulate</b> an emergency declaration <b>from legal  challenge</b></i>. If the president moves ahead with a controversial  plan to declare a national emergency as a way to free up money for construction  of a border wall, all eyes should be on Congress.   </p>
