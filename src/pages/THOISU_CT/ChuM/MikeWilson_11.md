  <p>1. Donald Trump không  phải là ứng viên TT đảng Cộng Hòa duy nhất 
    <i>có tư duy và chiến thuật kỳ thị chủng tộc</i> khi  ra tranh cử. 
  </p>
<p>
2. Ronald Reagan (Cộng Hòa), năm 1980, khi ra tranh cử TT Mỹ, 
   đã chọn <i>điểm khởi  hành</i> là Philadelphia, bang Mississippi, 
   <i>nơi mà trước đó 16 năm, đảng Klu Klux Klan đã giết hại  dã man 
   3 công dân tranh đấu cho dân quyền</i> (chống kỳ thị da  đen) 
</p>
<p>
3. <i>Reagan đến đó để rêu rao</i> "quyền của các bang" (states' right) 
   mà đó là <i>ẩn ngữ, tiếng lóng</i> (code word)  
   <i>dùng để chống phá quyền  lực của nhà nước liên bang 
   buộc các bang phải bảo vệ dân quyền và quyền đi bầu</i> 
  (của dân da đen) 
</p>
<p>
4. Trước đó dưới thời TT Richard Nixon, 
   đảng Cộng Hòa cũng dùng <i>"Chiến Lược Miền  Nam"</i> 
   qua các <i>ẩn ngữ và tiếng lóng</i> chính trị 
   để hốt phiếu từ các cử tri  da trắng <i>còn hậm hực  
   với dân quyền và quyền đi bầu của dân da đen</i></p>
  <p>5. Đây là <i>bước  ngoặt</i> đánh dấu sự chuyển hướng chính trị 
     của đa số dân da trắng miền Nam <i>từ đảng Dân Chủ sang  Cộng Hòa</i> 
  </p>
<p>   6. Quá trình lịch sử chính trị Mỹ cho thấy

     khi đảng này có lập trường cấp tiến ngày càng  giống 
     đảng Cộng Hòa nguyên thủy (giải phóng nô lệ) của  Abrahm Lincoln !</i></p>

   <p><i>An earlier Republican strategist under President Richard Nixon came up with the “Southern strategy,” which used certain code language to garner white votes in the Deep South by playing on lingering racial prejudice.    <p><b>Republicans (have been)  long on wrong side of racial history</b></p>
   <p>Donald Trump is probably  no more racist than&nbsp;<i>Ronald Reagan,  who, after gaining the Republican nomination in 1980, chose Philadelphia,  Miss., as his first campaign stop. This is the town where the Ku Klux Klan  savagely killed three civil rights workers in 1964</i>. When Reagan  spoke at that town — 16 years after the murders —&nbsp;<i>he emphasized his belief in “states’ rights.”</i> <br>
     <i>Back  then, “states’ rights” was code language that appealed to certain people who  were resentful of the strong reach of the federal government to put an end to  racial segregation in the Deep South. The people the term “states’ rights”  resonated with most strongly were those smarting over the Civil Rights Act of  1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.</i></p>
   <p><i>An  earlier Republican strategist under President Richard Nixon came up with the  “Southern strategy,” which used certain code language to garner white votes in  the Deep South by playing on lingering racial prejudice</i>.<b><i>&nbsp;This is the point when the Southern  white majority firmly switched its political allegiance from Democrat to  Republican.</i></b></p>
   <p>History shows that&nbsp;<b><i>white Southerners</i></b><i>&nbsp;gradually left the Democratic Party when it  started becoming more like the original (slave-freeing, anti-bigotry) party of  Abraham Lincoln</i>&nbsp;— the party that issued the Emancipation  Proclamation and amended the U.S. Constitution to give citizenship rights to  former slaves, suffrage to black men, and equal protection to all citizens  (including former slaves and their children). </p>
   <p>After World War II,&nbsp;<b><i>the  Democrats, under President Harry Truman, inserted into their platform a plank  for civil rights. This included integrating the U.S. military.&nbsp;</i></b>Such  bold action by the “liberal” Democrats caused consternation among Southern  Democratic leaders.<b><i>It led to a rebellion (from the South) and eventually  a switch of political parties.</i></b></p>
   <p>I doubt Trump is any  more racist than former Senate Majority Leader Trent Lott, a&nbsp;Republican  from Mississippi, who in 2002 regaled the memory of South Carolina Sen. Strom  Thurmond. This was during a 100th birthday celebration for Thurmond, who in  1948 had been the standard-bearer for the “<b><i>States’ Rights</i></b>&nbsp;Democratic  Party.” </p>
   <p>Lott recalled with pride  how his state had voted for Thurmond, adding “if the rest of the country had  followed our lead, we wouldn’t have had all these problems over the years,  either.”&nbsp;<i>Why did Thurmond run for  president representing the&nbsp;<b>“states’ rights”</b>&nbsp;third party? He  was displeased with Truman’s new agenda to promote<b>&nbsp;equal rights for  blacks.</b></i></p>
   <p>To his credit, President  George W. Bush denounced Lott’s statement on the 1948 election. But that does  not change<i>&nbsp;the fact of the 1988  election, when supporters of candidate George H.W. Bush resorted to<b>racist  tactics</b>&nbsp;by using the infamous Willie Horton ad (<b>Willie Horton</b>was  a&nbsp;<b>black convict</b>&nbsp;who committed<b>&nbsp;further crimes</b>&nbsp;while  he was temporarily released from jail . nth-fl).</i> <br>
     <i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willie_Horton" target="_blank">https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willie_Horton</a><br>
     </i></p>
   <p>All of this history  seems to be in harmony with those who have questioned&nbsp;<i>whether President Barack Obama was really born in  America. Trump is not the only Republican who has been obsessed by this  question on the origins of the first black president,&nbsp;<b>a question tinged  with racism</b></i>.&nbsp;(Obama was  born by a&nbsp;<b><i>white American-citizen</i></b>mother in Hawaii,&nbsp;<b><i>on  American territory</i></b>&nbsp;, and is no doubt&nbsp;<b><i>eligible</i></b>&nbsp;to  become an American president . nth-fl) </p>
   <p>If the Republican  establishment is serious about its contempt for Trump’s alleged racism, as well  as his less-than-kind words toward women or handicapped individuals, then it  can apologize for having been on the wrong side of history. <br>
     But as the famous Mississippian  (southern) author&nbsp;<b><i>William Faulkner</i></b>once wrote:&nbsp;<b><i>“The (Republican racist) past is&nbsp;never dead.  It’s not even past.”</i></b></p>
   <p>To kill this past will  require policy changes to match any anti-racist commitment. </p>
   <p>ROGER CHAPMAN, WEST PALM  BEACH</p>
