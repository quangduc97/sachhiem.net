<p>1. Nhân dân các nước Âu  Mỹ đang chịu sức ép&nbsp;<b><i>từ nhiều phía</i></b>&nbsp;:</p>
<p>  2. Ngoài sức ép&nbsp;<b><i>toàn cầu hóa </i></b>còn có sức ép của&nbsp;<b><i>di dân</i></b>&nbsp;vì lí do kinh tế và chiến tranh</p>
<p>  3. Toàn cầu hóa, qua khoán ngoại (outsourcing), lấy đi việc làm của dân - và  tạo nên các trung tâm sản xuất ngoài Âu Mỹ</p>
<p>  4. Di dân từ ngoài vào cũng lấy đi việc làm của dân, mặc dù phần lớn là việc  lao động và dịch vụ cấp thấp</p>
<p>  5. Cộng thêm vào đó,&nbsp; là căn&nbsp;<b><i>bệnh kinh niên</i></b>&nbsp;của tư bản chủ nghĩa, -&nbsp;<b><i>bòn rút tài sản lên  thượng tầng xã hội !</i></b></p>
<p>6. Hệ quả là&nbsp;<b><i>quốc dân nghèo đi</i></b>, <b><i>giai cấp trung lưu co cụm</i></b>,&nbsp;<b><i>giai cấp nghèo gia tăng, </i></b>và&nbsp;<b><i>phúc lợi của</i></b><b><i>&nbsp;</i></b><b><i>nhân</i></b><b><i>&nbsp;</i></b><b><i>dân bị cắt giảm hay gạt bỏ !</i></b> </p>
<p>  7. Trong  tương quan giữa hai thế lực tư bản và xã hội, thì&nbsp;<b><i>phe tư bản đang thắng thế</i></b>, để lại những&nbsp;<b><i>hậu quả tiêu cực</i></b>&nbsp;về mặt xã hội - khoan bàn đến  kinh tế và chính trị .</p>
<p>  8. Tầm nhìn tổng quan cho thấy, quốc gia nào có chính sách xã hội mạnh&nbsp; và&nbsp;<b><i>cân bằng</i></b>&nbsp;với chính sách tư bản, thì đời  sống nhân dân<b><i>&nbsp;</i></b><b><i>tương đối ổn định</i></b>&nbsp;và ít chênh lệch bất công - như  trường hợp các nước : Đan Mạch, Thụy Điển, Phần Lan, Canada nhưng trong các  nước Âu Mỹ khác khi thế lực tư bản<b><i>&nbsp;</i></b><b><i>dồn ép</i></b>&nbsp;thế lực xã hội thì&nbsp;<b><i>chênh lệch bất công nhiều hơn </i></b>-&nbsp; và&nbsp;<b><i>nhân dân bất mãn hơn</i></b></p>
<p>  9. Chính sự&nbsp;<b><i>bất mãn phổ quát</i></b>&nbsp;này thúc đẩy sự nổi lên của các&nbsp;<b><i>ứng viên quá khích </i></b>- hoặc là&nbsp;<b><i>cực tả (Bernie Sanders)</i></b>, hoặc là&nbsp;<b><i>cực hữu (Donald Trump)</i></b></p>
<p>  10. Hiện tượng này cũng cho thấy các đảng truyền thống đã&nbsp;<b><i>không giải quyết hiệu quả</i></b>&nbsp;các vấn nạn quốc gia, mà trái  lại, còn&nbsp;<b><i>gây ra chia rẽ, phân hóa, cực  đoan và quá khích</i></b></p>
<p>  11. Đây là bằng chứng rõ nhất, cho thấy&nbsp;<b><i>đa đảng,</i></b>&nbsp;theo thể chế&nbsp;<b><i>dân chủ</i></b>&nbsp;<b><i>ngụy</i></b>&nbsp;Âu Mỹ, đang đi vào&nbsp;<b><i>ngõ cụt</i></b><b><i>,</i></b>&nbsp;và&nbsp;<b><i>không hề thành công như quảng cáo</i></b></p>

<p><a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/trumpism-made-in-europe/2016/05/25/b83fd94a-22a2-11e6-9e7f-57890b612299_story.html" target="_blank">Trumpism: Made in Europe</a></p>
<p><a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/trumpism-made-in-europe/2016/05/25/b83fd94a-22a2-11e6-9e7f-57890b612299_story.html">https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/trumpism-made-in-europe/2016/05/25/b83fd94a-22a2-11e6-9e7f-57890b612299_story.html</a><br>
  <br>
  Here’s the irony of Donald Trump’s “America  First,” immigrant-bashing, free-trade-averse,  make-us-great-again nationalism: It is a European import.<br>
  <br>
  The American right  has typically been anti-government, reverent of the Constitution, suspicious of  political strongmen and resolute in insisting that “American exceptionalism” makes us different from other nations.<br>
  <br>
  But Trumpism is not  an American original. Almost every plank in the candidate’s vaguely defined platform is derivative of  the European far right. It is gaining ground on the basis of opposition to  immigration, fears of terrorism and crime, economic nationalism, and promises  of a government wielding a muscular hand against the forces of disorder.<br>
  <br>
  While one would  like to think that the copycat nature of Trump’s ideology will, in the coming months, make  it increasingly less attractive to American voters, his rise is no less disturbing  for being emblematic of what’s  happening across so many democracies.<br>
  <br>
  Trump’s emergence is a symptom of a larger  democratic distemper roiling the world’s political parties on the center-right and center-left that have  underwritten free government since 1945.<br>
  <br>
  For all their  differences, these parties have shared a commitment to institutions that  combined liberty with welfare; created a reasonably well-distributed  prosperity; respected the power of democratic government to do good but also  accepted its limits; and embraced the need for compromise.<br>
  <br>
  The weakness of  these parties was brought home dramatically this week in Austria where Norbert  Hofer, the candidate of the far-right Freedom Party that has explicit roots in  the Nazi past, nearly won the country’s presidency.<br>
  <br>
  Yes, it was good  news that Hofer was edged out by Alexander Van der Bellen, who was backed by  the Green Party. But Van der Bellen’s margin was unsettlingly small &nbsp;he won 50.3 percent of the vote to Hofer’s 49.7 percent.<br>
  <br>
  The fact that the alternative  to the far right came from the Greens reflected the decline of the two parties  dominant in Austrian politics since World War II. The candidates of the  center-right People’s  Party and the center-left Social Democrats didn’t even make the runoff. Between them, they  mustered only 22.4 percent in the first round of voting. Imagine a U.S.  election in which Republicans and Democrats were, together, reduced to little  more than one-fifth of the total.<br>
  <br>
  The voting patterns  in Austria closely resembled those visible on our side of the Atlantic. Polls  commissioned by ORF, Austria’s  public broadcaster, showed that Hofer (like Trump in the primaries and in the  polls) led handily in rural areas, among men and among manual workers. Van der  Bellen swamped the right-wing candidate in the big cities and among women,  while also leading him among white-collar workers.<br>
  <br>
  Mainstream parties,  which can be infected by complacency, certainly bear some responsibility for  what’s happening. The defection of working-class  voters to the far right is a cross-democracy electoral phenomenon that reflects  a serious failure on the part of social democratic and progressive parties  whose historical task had been to represent citizens in blue collars.<br>
  <br>
  At the same time,  the moderate conservative parties have seen some of their own natural  constituents drawn away by rising anti-immigrant feeling -- this has hurt German Chancellor Angela  Merkel’s Christian Democratic Union -- aggravated by Europe’s refugee crisis.<br>
  <br>
  Here again, the  Trump analogy holds: Mainstream Republicans winked and nodded toward a hard  line on immigration; Trump has embraced it whole with his calls for a border  wall and a temporary ban on admitting Muslims to the country.<br>
  Thus another  cross-Atlantic similarity: Opinions that were once far outside the normal  political discourse on immigration and nationalism are now expressed routinely.  Katya Adler, the BBC’s  Europe editor, captured this trend by pointing to the German word “salonfahig,” which literally means “passable for your living room,” i.e., socially acceptable.<br>
  <br>
  Trump’s relentless attacks on “political correctness” are intended to break the barriers against  what had once been beyond-the-pale sentiments on immigrants and race. His crude  approach to campaigning (on Tuesday, he called Hillary Clinton “this low life”) reflects an indifference to norms that  reinforces popular contempt for politics and traditional politicians.<br>
  <br>
  Standing up against  the new far right should be a shared task across the old political divides in all  democracies. But Republican politicians are falling in line one by one behind  Trump, choosing to ignore the threat he poses to political decency and his  challenge to democratic values themselves.<br>
  <br>
  The United States  should not look to the European far right as our model. The land of opportunity  and freedom with a long tradition of welcoming newcomers should be leading the  resistance to the new authoritarianism.<br>
