	<p>For the ailing John Paul II, in the twilight of his papacy, it has been a 
	distressing time. </p>
	<p>What turned into a global crisis for the church began with a spate of 
	allegations in the United States. </p>
	<p>When one former priest went on trial in Boston, victims of sexual abuse 
	in churches across the country started hiring lawyers. </p>
	<p>In the space of a few weeks, the accusations - some stretching back many 
	years - multiplied at an alarming rate. </p>
	<p><b>Resignation demands</b> </p>
	<p>Particularly damaging for the Church were claims that bishops had tried 
	to keep a lid on the scandal by quietly moving abusing priests from one 
	diocese to another. </p>
	<p>But now it was all out in the open. Priests were suspended, bishops 
	resigned, and cardinals faced demands for their resignation. </p>
	<p>The US cardinals were summoned to Rome for a crisis meeting with the 
	Pope. By the end of the year, a policy had been agreed for dealing with 
	suspected paedophiles. </p>
	<p>But the Vatican's insistence on safeguards for those accused led to 
	complaints by victims' groups that the promised "zero tolerance" policy was 
	being watered down. </p>
	<p>By now the US Church found itself facing multi-million dollar lawsuits 
	from hundreds of Catholics claiming to have been abused by priests. </p>
	<p>Alarmed by the financial consequences, church leaders in Boston 
	considered filing for bankruptcy. But it was the growing damage to the 
	reputation of the church that caused most concern. </p>
	<p>And so the city's archbishop, Cardinal Bernard Law, returned to the 
	Vatican to discuss his future. </p>
	<p><b>Global scandal </b></p>
	<p><b></b>With many of his own priests calling for him to stand down, his 
	position had become untenable. This time the Pope accepted his resignation.
	</p>
	<p>It would have been bad enough if the scandal had been confined to the US, 
	a relatively small part of a global church with one billion members. </p>
	<p>But the crisis focused attention on allegations being made across the 
	Catholic world, from Brazil to Hong Kong, from Ireland to South Africa. </p>
	<p>In Poland, homeland of the Pope, an archbishop stood accused of molesting 
	young men training for the priesthood. </p>

	of "sexual misconduct". </p>
	<p>In Australia, the church took out newspaper advertisements to apologise 
	to victims of sex abuse by its priests. </p>
	<p>And in the United Kingdom, the Archbishop of Westminster, Cormac 
	Murphy-O'Connor, faced awkward questions about the way he had dealt with an 
	abusing priest. </p>
	<p>In country after country, a scandal that had existed just beneath the 
	surface of church life burst into the headlines. </p>
	<p>It forced the Vatican to confront the issue of sexual abuse by priests, 
	and the consequences could be far reaching. </p>
	<p>The spotlight is on gay men in the church, and many innocent priests feel 
	they are now under suspicion. </p>
	<p>They fear there will now be efforts to stop gay men joining the clergy. 
	But in many countries, it is already difficult finding enough recruits for 
	the priesthood. </p>
	<p>This has been a painful issue for the Catholic Church to confront. </p>
	<p>But the Pope knows that the concerns of the faithful must be addressed if 
	trust in the priesthood is to be restored. </p>

	<p>But the way senior members of the church tried to keep it quiet took the
