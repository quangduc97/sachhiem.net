<p>In a recent&nbsp;<a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2020/04/01/andrew-yang-coronavirus-discrimination/"><b>Washington Post op-ed</b></a>,  former Democratic presidential candidate Andrew Yang called upon Asian  Americans to become part of the solution against COVID-19.<br>
  In the face of rising  anti-Asian racist actions – now at about&nbsp;<a href="https://thehill.com/changing-america/respect/equality/490373-attacks-on-asian-americans-at-about-100-per-day-due-to"><b>100 reported cases per day</b></a>&nbsp;–  Yang implores Asian Americans to “wear red, white, and blue” in their efforts  to combat the virus.<br>
  Optimistically, before  Donald Trump declared COVID-19 as the “Chinese virus,” Yang believed that  “getting the virus under control” would rid this country of its anti-Asian  racism. But Asian American history,&nbsp;<a href="https://dornsife.usc.edu/cf/ase/faculty_display.cfm?person_id=1091464"><b>my field of research</b></a>,  suggests a sobering reality.<br>
  <b>A history of anti-Asian  racism</b><br>
  Up until the eve of  the COVID-19 crisis, the&nbsp;<a href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2018/jul/28/being-a-good-quiet-and-assimilated-model-minority-is-making-me-angry"><b>prevailing narrative</b></a>&nbsp;about  Asian Americans was one of the model minority.<br>
  The model minority  concept, developed during and after World War II, posits that Asian Americans  were the ideal immigrants of color to the United States due to their economic  success.<br>
  But in the United  States, Asian Americans have long been considered as a threat to a nation that  promoted a whites-only immigration policy. They were called a “yellow  peril”:&nbsp;<a href="https://theconversation.com/who-is-born-a-us-citizen-127403"><b>unclean and unfit for  citizenship in America</b></a>.<br>
  In the late 19th  century, white nativists spread xenophobic propaganda about Chinese  uncleanliness in San Francisco. This fueled the passage of the infamous&nbsp;<a href="https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2020-02-03/coronavirus-outbreak-narrative-xenophobia"><b>Chinese Exclusion Act</b></a>,  the first law in the United States that barred immigration solely based on  race. Initially, the act placed a 10-year moratorium on all Chinese migration.<br>
  In the early 20th  century, American officials in the Philippines, then a formal colony of the  U.S., denigrated&nbsp;<a href="https://www.dukeupress.edu/colonial-pathologies"><b>Filipinos for their supposedly  unclean and uncivilized bodies</b></a>. Colonial officers and doctors  identified two enemies: Filipino insurgents against American rule, and  “tropical diseases” festering in native bodies. By pointing to Filipinos’  political and medical unruliness, these officials justified continued U.S.  colonial rule in the islands.<br>
  On February 19, 1942,  President Franklin Delano Roosevelt signed&nbsp;<a href="https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/fdr-signs-executive-order-9066"><b>Executive Order 9066</b></a>&nbsp;to  incarcerate people under suspicion as enemies to inland internment camps.<br>
  While the order also  affected German- and Italian-Americans on the East Coast, the vast majority of  those incarcerated in 1942 were of Japanese descent. Many of them were  naturalized citizens, second- and third-generation Americans. Internees who  fought in the celebrated 442nd Regiment were coerced by the United States  military to prove their loyalty to a country that locked them up simply for  being Japanese.<br>
  In the 21st century,  even the most “multicultural” North American cities, like my hometown of  Toronto, Canada, are hotbeds for virulent racism. During the 2003 SARS  outbreak, Toronto saw a rise of&nbsp;<a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/outlook/2020/02/04/2003-sars-outbreak-fueled-anti-asian-racism-this-pandemic-doesnt-have/"><b>anti-Asian racism</b></a>,  much like that of today.<br>
  In her 2008 study,  sociologist Carrianne Leung highlights&nbsp;<a href="https://link.gale.com/apps/doc/A195680111/AONE?u=aikentcl&amp;sid=AONE&amp;xid=65126ad5"><b>the everyday racism against  Chinese and Filipina health care workers</b></a>&nbsp;in the years that  followed the SARS crisis. While publicly celebrated for their work in hospitals  and other health facilities, these women found themselves fearing for their  lives on their way home.<br>
  No expression of  patriotism – not even being front-line workers in a pandemic – makes Asian  migrants immune to&nbsp;<a href="https://www.huffpost.com/entry/asian-american-racism-coronavirus_l_5e790a71c5b63c3b64954eb4"><b>racism</b></a>.<br>
  <b>Making the model minority</b><br>
  Over the past decade,  from&nbsp;<a href="https://www.pulitzer.org/winners/viet-thanh-nguyen"><b>Pulitzer Prizes</b></a>&nbsp;to&nbsp;<a href="https://www.imdb.com/title/tt3104988/"><b>popular films</b></a>, Asian Americans have slowly been  gaining&nbsp;<a href="https://www.npr.org/2020/01/19/797721961/asian-americans-in-hollywood"><b>better representation</b></a>&nbsp;in  Hollywood and other cultural industries.<br>
  Whereas “The Joy Luck  Club” had long been the most infamous depiction of Asian-ness in Hollywood, by  the 2018 Golden Globes, Sandra Oh declared her now famous adage:&nbsp;<a href="https://i-d.vice.com/en_us/article/xwjbw7/why-sandra-ohs-speech-at-the-golden-globes-meant-so-much-to-me"><b>“It’s an honor just to be  Asian.”</b></a>&nbsp;It was, at least at face value, a moment of  cultural inclusion.<br>
  However, so-called  Asian American inclusion has a dark side.<br>
  In reality, as&nbsp;<a href="https://tupress.temple.edu/book/3393"><b>cultural historian Robert G. Lee has argued</b></a>,  inclusion can and has been used to undermine the activism of African Americans,  indigenous peoples and other marginalized groups in the United States. In the  words of writer Frank Chin in 1974,&nbsp;<a href="https://uwapress.uw.edu/book/9780295746487/aiiieeeee/"><b>“Whites love us because we’re  not black.”</b></a><br>
  For example, in 1943,  a year after the United States incarcerated Japanese Americans under Executive  Order 9066, Congress repealed the Chinese Exclusion Act. White liberals  advocated for the repeal not out of altruism toward Chinese migrants, but to  advocate for a&nbsp;<a href="https://www.latimes.com/opinion/op-ed/la-oe-0123-wu-chua-model-minority-chinese-20140123-story.html"><b>transpacific alliance against  Japan and the Axis powers</b></a>.<br>
  By allowing for the  free passage of Chinese migrants to the United States, the nation could show  its supposed fitness as an interracial superpower that rivaled Japan and  Germany. Meanwhile, incarcerated Japanese Americans in camps and African  Americans were still held under Jim Crow segregation laws.<br>
  In her new book, “<a href="https://uncpress.org/book/9781469653365/opening-the-gates-to-asia/"><b>Opening the Gates to Asia: A  Transpacific History of How America Repealed Asian Exclusion</b></a>,”  Occidental College historian Jane Hong reveals how the United States government  used Asian immigration inclusion against other minority groups at a time of  social upheaval.<br>
  For example, in 1965,  Lyndon B. Johnson’s administration signed the much-celebrated&nbsp;<a href="https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1951-2000/Immigration-and-Nationality-Act-of-1965/"><b>Hart-Celler Act</b></a>&nbsp;into  law. The act primarily targeted Asian and African migrants, shifting  immigration from an exclusionary quota system to an merit-based points system.  However, it also imposed immigration restrictions on Latin America.<br>
  <b>Beyond model minority  politics</b><br>
  As history shows,  Asian American communities stand to gain more working within communities and  across the lines of race, rather than trying to appeal to those in power.<br>
  Japanese American activists  such as the late&nbsp;<a href="https://www.npr.org/sections/codeswitch/2014/06/02/318072652/japanese-american-activist-and-malcolm-x-ally-dies-at-93"><b>Yuri Kochiyama</b></a>&nbsp;worked  in solidarity with other communities of color to advance the civil rights movement.<br>
  A former internee at  the Jerome Relocation Center in Arkansas, Kochiyama’s postwar life in Harlem,  and her friendship with Malcolm X, inspired her to become active in the  anti-Vietnam War and civil rights movements. In the 1980s, she and her husband  Bill, himself part of the 442nd Regiment, worked at the forefront of the  reparations and apology movement for Japanese internees. As a result of their  efforts,&nbsp;<a href="https://psmag.com/social-justice/japanese-americans-fight-for-post-internment-reparations-offers-a-blueprint-for-fighting-inequality-in-the-trump-era"><b>Ronald Reagan signed the  resulting Civil Liberties Act into law in 1988</b></a>.<br>
  Kochiyama and  activists like her have inspired the cross-community work of Asian American  communities after them.<br>
  In Los Angeles, where  I live, the Little Tokyo Service Center is among those at the forefront of  grassroots&nbsp;<a href="https://hyperallergic.com/453166/artists-are-addressing-the-tide-of-gentrification-in-las-little-tokyo/"><b>organizing for affordable  housing and social services in a rapidly gentrifying neighborhood</b></a>.  While the organization’s priority area is Little Tokyo and its community  members, the center’s work advocates for affordable housing among black and  Latinx residents, as well as Japanese American and other Asian American groups.<br>
  To the northwest in  Koreatown, the grassroots organization Ktown for All conducts outreach to  unhoused residents of the neighborhood, regardless of ethnic background.<br>
  The coronavirus sees  no borders. Likewise, I think that everyone must follow the example of these  organizations and activists, past and present, to reach across borders and  contribute to collective well-being.<br>
  Self-isolation, social  distancing and healthy practices should not be in the service of proving one’s  patriotism. Instead, these precautions should be done for the sake of caring  for those whom we do and do not know, inside and outside our national  communities.<br>
