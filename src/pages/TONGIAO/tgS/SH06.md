Đây chỉ là những đồ biểu, một dữ liệu cần thiết để đo lường trạng thái sinh hoạt của một định chế đã và đang chi phối đời sống tinh thần của một số đông người trên thế giới.  Người có trách nhiệm sẽ phải can đảm đối diện với kết quả của hệ thống giáo luật áp đặt cho giới tu sĩ. Có lẽ đã đến lúc Vatican cần phải thay đổi qui chế, thay vì bưng bít một chuyện càng ngày càng nát bấy và đổ bể. </i> </p>

	<p class="TuaDoLeft">1. Catholic church hit with another abuse claim </p>
	<p>ABC News, Australia/October 29, 2009 </p>
	<p>By Gus Goswell for PM </p>
	<p>The Roman Catholic clergy is under fire over a Victorian woman's 
	accusation that a Catholic priest in Ballarat sexually abused her when she 
	was seven years old. </p>
	<p>Her lawyers sent a letter outlining the woman's claims to every parish in 
	Victoria, asking that it be read to all Victorian congregations. </p>
	<p>The alleged victim says she was sexually abused by Gerald Ridsdale, a 
	former priest who is currently serving a prison sentence for abusing more 
	than 20 children. </p>
	<p>But Ballarat's Bishop says he is convinced the priest did not sexually 
	abuse the woman and the Catholic Archbishop Denis Hart has instructed 
	Melbourne's priests to ignore the letter. </p>
	<p>A warning: this report contains graphic allegations. </p>
	<p>She recounted the alleged abuse on Fairfax Radio in Melbourne this 
	morning. </p>
	<p>"I was made to drink my own urine, eat my own vomit. I was beaten many 
	times," she said. </p>
	<p>"Yes, by Ridsdale, I was [sexually assaulted], yes." </p>
	<p>The woman says her abuse began in the 1960s when she was seven years old 
	at an orphanage run by the Catholic church in the regional Victorian city of 
	Ballarat. </p>
	<p>That claim is disputed by Ballarat's Bishop, Peter Connors, who says he 
	is convinced the woman's version of events is wrong. </p>
	<p>Nonetheless, she was given a $12,000 payment several years ago by the 
	organisation that ran the home. </p>
	<p>A spokeswoman for The Sisters of Nazareth has told the ABC the payment 
	was a compassionate one or, in her words, "a pastoral gesture", not a 
	compensation payment. </p>
	<p>The Catholic Church says $12,000 was not related to the woman's current 
	claims. </p>
	<p>Angela Sdrinis, the alleged victim's lawyer, has sent a letter detailing 
	her client's abuse claims to every Victorian parish priest, to point out 
	that the Catholic Church's legal structure makes adequate compensation 
	almost impossible to obtain. </p>
	<p>"The Catholic Church, they've been hiding behind what I could call a 
	corporate veil for over a decade," she said. </p>
	<p>"In the cases of historical sexual abuse, the head of the church or the 
	religious order in question has often passed away, they no longer exist. </p>
	<p>"That leaves the property trust as the only legal entity that can be 
	sued, and the High Court agreed with the argument of the Catholic Church 
	that as the property trust has nothing to do with the hiring and firing and 
	supervision of priests, they can't be held liable in cases of sexual abuse.
	</p>
	<p>"So that leaves victims with no-one to sue." </p>
	<p>The ABC has seen a copy of a letter Archbishop Dennis Hart wrote to 
	Melbourne's parish priests in response to Ms Sdrinis' letter. </p>
	<p>In it, Archbishop Hart says the claims of abuse have no connection to the 
	Archdiocese of Melbourne and that a complaint in relation to the abuse has 
	already been dealt with. </p>
	<p>Archbishop Hart goes on to say that it is inappropriate for the letter's 
	contents to be shared in the parishes of the Melbourne Archdiocese. </p>
	<p>'Make it easier' for victims</p>
	<p>But Chris MacIsaac, who leads Broken Rights - a support group for people 
	sexually abused by Catholic clergy - says the Catholic church needs to make 
	it easier for victims of abuse to pursue their claims. </p>
	<p>"It really boils down to the fact they feel they've been treated unjustly 
	and they feel that they've been denied their day in court," she said. </p>
	<p>"It is very fragmented and it's very difficult to know who can be sued 
	and who can't, and at the end of the day, victims feel that they've used 
	everything in their power to keep them away from a fair and just outcome."
	</p>
	<p>Father Brian Lucas from the Australian Catholic Bishops Conference 
	disputes that. </p>
	<p>"The Towards Healing program that is used by most of the dioceses and 
	religious orders in Australia and the Melbourne protocol itself make 
	provision to deal pastorally with people who make these allegations and in 
	the appropriate cases that can involve compensation," he said. </p>
	<p>"That is an alternative to litigation. The suggestion that the church 
	can't be sued is simply not correct." </p>
	<p>Father Lucas says he does not believe the woman who spoke out today was 
	abused by Ridsdale at the Ballarat orphanage. </p>
	<p>"The allegations were referred to the Victoria police who did not take 
	any action," he said. </p>
	<p>"But apart from that, the church's own internal process under the Towards 
	Healing protocol involved an independent assessment that was very thoroughly 
	undertaken and in the particular case it was found that there were 
	inconsistencies in the way that the story was being presented which 
	suggested it lacked credibility. </p>
	<p>"There was then an appeal against that and the review panel reviewed the 
	assessment and came to the same conclusion." </p>
	<p>The alleged victim says she intends to continue pursuing a civil action 
	anyway. </p>
	<p class="TuaDoLeft"> 2.  4 Amish bishops charged with not reporting abuse </p>
	<p>The Associated Press/November 3, 2009 </p>
	<p>By Maria Sudekum Fisher </p>
	<p>Marshfield, Mo. - Four Amish leaders in southwest Missouri who chose to 
	"shun" an accused child molester in their community rather than report him 
	to authorities were charged Tuesday with failing to report the sexual abuse.
	</p>
	<p>Webster County prosecutor Danette Padgett said all four are bishops and 
	face one misdemeanor count each of failure to report child abuse as a 
	mandatory reporter. </p>
	<p>Under Missouri law, people with "responsibility for the care of children" 
	are required to report suspected child abuse. Examples of mandatory 
	reporters under the statute are doctors, nurses, social workers and teachers 
	and ministers who are not engaged in a "privileged communication." </p>
	<p>The bishops were identified as Emmanuel M.S. Eicher, 44, Peter M. Eicher, 
	59, Jacob P. Schwartz, 79, and Christian J.F. Schwartz, 41, all of rural 
	Webster County. </p>
	<p>An attorney for the men, Will Worsham, questioned whether they can be 
	considered mandatory reporters. He said the Amish do not separate government 
	and religion and that a bishop is akin to a mayor or city councilman. </p>
	<p>"I'm not convinced 'bishop' necessarily implies any type of religious 
	authority in their community. And even if it did, I'm not sure they would 
	qualify as 'clergy.' It doesn't appear anywhere in the law." </p>
	<p>The four men knew that a member of their community, Johnny A. Schwartz, 
	36, had been abusing two underage children from June 2007 through June 2008, 
	according to the probable cause statement. Schwartz was charged in 
	mid-October with six counts involving sexual abuse of children. </p>
	<p>Authorities would not say how Schwartz and the bishops with the same 
	surname are related. They also would not release the ages of the children or 
	their relationship to Johnny Schwartz. </p>
	<p>About 2,200 Amish live in Webster County. Sheriff Roye H. Cole said 
	authorities found out about the alleged abuse from someone who works among 
	the them. That individual, who has not been identified, had heard about 
	Schwartz being shunned by the community and asked why. </p>
	<p>Shunning is a form of punishment among the Amish in which a member of the 
	group who fails to follow the rules is ostracized. </p>
	<p>"They make the religious argument that they've punished them," Cole said. 
	"They're shunned, and if they confessed their sins and ask forgiveness, they 
	have no moral authority to report it to the law." </p>
	<p>"They feel they cannot report a brother that has been forgiven of their 
	sins," he added. </p>
	<p>Padgett and Cole said they believe the bishops were told about the 
	alleged abuse by Schwartz's wife, which would make it a nonprivileged 
	communication because she was not "confessing." </p>
	<p>But Worsham, who also represents Johnny Schwartz, said prosecutors are 
	using an overly broad definition of the mandatory reporter law. </p>
	<p>"In order to hold up charges against these individuals, it could make a 
	lot of people 'mandatory reporters' in this state that would be shocked to 
	learn they were defined as such," Worsham said. "If you're a part-time 
	Sunday school teacher, you may suddenly be defined as a 'mandatory 
	reporter.' I don't think that's what anyone has contemplated in the past."
	</p>
	<p>Associated Press Writer Dana Fields in Kansas City, Mo., also contributed 
	to this report. </p>
	
	<p class="TuaDoLeft">3. Chicago Archdiocese settles abuse 
	cases for $3.9 million </p>
	<p>Catholic News Service/July 25, 2009 </p>
	<p>Chicago -- The Archdiocese of Chicago announced July 21 that it had 
	reached settlements totaling $3.9 million in cases of six survivors of 
	clergy sex abuse. All of the cases involved abuse that happened between 1970 
	and 1986, and all of the priests involved have been out of ministry for 
	almost 20 years, the archdiocese said. Two of the priests are dead; the 
	other two have been laicized. </p>
	<p>The announcement said the archdiocese and the survivors reached an 
	agreement through "a comprehensive mediation process," which included 
	sharing documents and information as well as the public disclosure of 
	information. The parties involved also used an arbitrator to resolve any 
	disputes. "It has long been the process of the archdiocese to reach out to 
	victims to try to resolve their claims without requiring them to go through 
	lengthy court proceedings," archdiocesan chancellor Jimmy Lago said in a 
	statement. "We believe that negotiation and mediation are the best, most 
	compassionate ways to resolve these cases, as it spares the survivors and 
	their families the burdens and stresses of an extended legal process," he 
	added. </p>

	<p class="TuaDoLeft">5. Six decades later, 2 men accuse 
	nuns of sex abuse </p>
	<p>Star-Ledger, New Jersey/October 04, 2009 </p>
	<p>From a sidewalk in Kearny, Henry Coffey, 80, points to the sections of 
	the old Sacred Heart Orphanage where he says nuns molested and beat him as a 
	boy in the early 1940s: the laundry room; the nuns' quarters; the 
	classrooms. </p>
	<p>His sex-abuse claims will be hard to prove in court -- the nuns are 
	deceased, for one thing - but Coffey and another former orphanage resident, 
	Frank Fioretti, 81, recently cleared a legal hurdle when a Superior Court 
	judge in Essex County denied a motion to dismiss their 2005 suit. </p>
	<p>That motion had been brought by the Pallatine Sisters, the religious 
	order now based in Harriman, N.Y., that ran the boys orphanage until it 
	closed in the 1950s. </p>
	<p>"I remember everything," Coffey said in an interview during which he 
	recounted allegations that a nun and he had repeated sexual encounters in 
	her room during his seventh-grade year, and intercourse at a bungalow in 
	Harriman during the summer. "I always seemed to have a good memory." </p>
	<p>Allegations that Catholic clergy molested minors in the 1960s, 1970s and 
	1980s have become common in the last seven years, in the aftermath of the 
	clergy sex scandal. Allegations dating to the 1940s are rarer, said Jeff 
	Anderson, an attorney in St. Paul, Minn., who has represented thousands in 
	clergy sex-abuse cases. </p>
	<p>"You just don't hear about it," he said, "because those people have 
	learned to suffer in silence and are afraid of rebuke, reprisal or 
	disbelief." </p>
	<p>Matters of time</p>
	<p>They present obvious challenges to the court system, as the defense 
	pointed out while trying to dismiss the case: Witnesses die. Evidence is 
	lost. Memories fade. And then there are the statutes of limitations. </p>
	<p>Stressing the passage of time since the alleged abuse, the Pallatines' 
	attorneys noted in court papers that when the plaintiffs lived at the 
	orphanage, from 1937 to 1943, "Franklin D. Roosevelt was the president of 
	the United States, Winston Churchill was the British prime minister, the 
	Japanese attacked Pearl Harbor and James Cagney won the 1942 Academy Award 
	for best actor for his role in the classic movie "Yankee Doodle Dandy.'" </p>
	<p>The Pallatines' lawyer, in pressing for dismissal, argued that statutes 
	of limitations were in effect, that the plaintiffs' delays in suing make the 
	case virtually impossible to defend, and that Coffey tried to unfairly 
	influence the testimony of another former orphanage resident. The lawyer, 
	Anthony Dougherty, did not return calls seeking comment. </p>
	



	<p class="TuaDoLeft">6. Clergy sex abuse: Ex-priest Daniel McCormack seeks 
	to seal records in commitment battle </p>
	<p>State says he is likely to commit another sex crime; officials want to 
	extend his incarceration</p>
	<p>Chicago Tribune/October 20, 2009 </p>
	<p>By Manya A. Brachear </p>
	<p>Attorneys representing a convicted former priest moved on Monday to keep 
	private most of the court proceedings and seal the records related to an 
	effort to have him committed under a state statute for sex offenders. </p>
	<p>Under the Sexually Violent Persons Commitment Act, the Illinois attorney 
	general and the Cook County state's attorney filed a joint petition last 
	month to have Daniel McCormack confined to a state treatment facility. The 
	law allows prosecutors to seek continued incarceration if a psychological 
	exam leads them to believe another sex crime is likely if the inmate goes 
	free. </p>
	<p>Last month, a forensic psychiatrist diagnosed McCormack with pedophilia 
	and recommended civil commitment, a recommendation attached to the joint 
	petition. </p>
	<p>Citing details in that publicized report, defense attorney Daniel Coyne, 
	a professor at Chicago-Kent College of Law, asked Judge Dennis Porter to 
	seal records going forward and hear testimony about McCormack's mental 
	health behind closed doors. </p>
	<p>"Continued dissemination of the respondent's past crimes, current mental 
	state, mental health history and psychiatric diagnosis to the Cook County 
	jury pool will pose a serious and imminent threat to the fairness of the 
	commitment proceedings," the motion said. </p>
	<p>Prosecutors declined to respond to the motions before the next hearing 
	date Nov. 4. </p>
	<p>Barbara Blaine, president of the Survivors Network of those Abused by 
	Priests, objected to the defense team's efforts to keep part of the 
	proceedings private, saying McCormack gave up his claims to privacy "when he 
	chose to molest boys and devastate young lives." </p>
	<p>Officials with the Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Chicago declined to 
	comment on Monday's proceedings but reiterated that McCormack was removed 
	from the priesthood in 2007 by Vatican decree. </p>
	<p>Mark Heyrman, clinical professor of law at the University of Chicago, 
	said that if civil commitment is not considered extra punishment for the 
	crimes committed, McCormack has a right to request protection. </p>
	<p>"In my view, there aren't any good reasons why this hearing should be 
	open to the public if the defendant doesn't want it open," he said. "Why do 
	we need to know what's going on in the actual hearing? The fact that it's 
	public may affect the outcome." </p>
	<p>McCormack, 40, pleaded guilty in July 2007 to abusing five boys and was 
	sentenced to five years in prison. </p>
	



	<p class="TuaDoLeft">7. RC priest jailed 30 years for sodomy </p>
	<p>The Guardian, UK/August 10, 2006 </p>
<p>
	By Rose Mirondo </p>
	<p>A Roman Catholic Priest, Sixtus Kimaro, was yesterday sentenced to 30 
	years in jail for sodomy. </p>
	<p>Kimaro, 38, was further ordered by Kisutu Resident Magistrate’s court in 
	Dar es Salaam to pay 2m/- in compensation to the 17-year youth he was found 
	guilty of sodomising. </p>
		<p>In passing the sentence, Principal Resident Magistrate Pellagia 
		Khaday, noted that the long jail sentence was intended to be a deterrent 
		to potential offenders. </p>
		<p>In her judgement, Khaday said the prosecution had proved in court 
		beyond doubt that the youth (name withheld) was indeed sodomised. </p>
		<p>The magistrate said it had been proved that Kimaro had unnatural 
		sexual intercourse with the youth, who is still considered a minor in 
		law and, therefore, incapable informed consent. </p>
		<p>She said that the reverend indecently assaulted the youth and 
		dehumanised him. </p>
		<p>”In view of the strong and compelling evidence tendered, I have no 
		doubt whatsoever in my mind that the prosecution has proved its case 
		beyond any reasonable shadow of doubt. </p>
		<p>I therefore convict you,” the magistrate said. </p>
		<p>The prosecutor, Willy Mlulu, noted: ”The accused being a clergyman 
		ought to have been a role model. </p>
		<p>I therefore ask the court to sentence him to life imprisonment for 
		the first count and five years for the second count. </p>
		<p>He should also be ordered to compensate the victim for the social 
		embarrassment, physical and psychological torture he inflicted on him,’’ 
		Mlulu said. </p>
		<p>In mitigation, the convict’s lawyer, Dr Masumbuko Lamwai, asked the 
		court to exercise leniency. </p>
		<p>”Your honour, my client is a first offender. Moreover, if imprisoned, 
		he stands to lose his reverend status,’’ Dr Lamwai told the court. </p>
		<p>Having heard the mitigation, the magistrate handed Rev Kimaro a 
		30-year custodial sentence for sodomy and five years for dehumanisation 
		of the youth. The sentences are to run concurrently. </p>
